Bachelor's Japanese resources, last updated 9/6/26
I taught myself to read, write and speak Japanese in less than a year using the resources I'll talk about here; much of this is adapted from my semi(?)-viral(?) Tumblr post on the topic, much of which was inspired by my discovery of learnjapanese.moe, another truly great site. The webmaster has a lot of expertise and experience that I don't have, so I suggest you check out some of her resources as well. I also love kuri's immersion guide, a page similar to this one.

Resources in bold are my personal favorites. I provide several alternatives when available; just pick your favorite.

A few minor tips from me:
  1. On the Topic of Isolated Kanji Study
  2. Most people aim for a 7:3 reading to listening ratio. Mine is about 8:2. Try to find something that works for you, but don't totally write off one or the other! You'll probably regret not knowing how to read if all you can do is listen, and you'll probably regret not knowing how to listen if all you can do is read. Speaking and writing are their own skills, but usually come as a consequence of knowing both of the former.
  3. You don't have to read/watch things exactly at your difficulty level. My first full VN was long (650k?) and way above my level (equivalent to about N2 comprehension); the first half was super difficult for me because I had never read before, but by the end of it, I had made massive gains. Obviously, I'm not telling you to immediately read Muramasa or anything, but motivation can take you to great places. If you have the hardheadedness and patience to push through, consider picking a harder title you're absolutely psyched about over an easier one that you don't really care for.
  4. Note that easier immersion material tends to be in the "cute girls doing cute things" genre. Darker themes and complex concepts typically go hand in hand with an increase in difficulty. That said, some people just really can't stand moe or need suggestions from other places: with people like you in mind, I've made my own VN difficulty list (linked below) with some easy-ish, darker/murdery/psychological picks. You can also utilize my guestbook (or email me at moemeter@proton.me) if you need personalized recs/have a more involved question to pick my brain about.
  5. Regarding mining: I recommend mining anything at around 10000 frequency and below. This sounds like a lot; it's really not. Try not to mine things like hyper-specific jargon unless it pertains to your interests. You don't need to know everything, especially as a beginner, and this is a good way to overwhelm yourself with words you don't need. Also, don't be afraid to delete and remake your Anki decks every now and then. I've deleted my mining deck before and started anew because my first mining deck was full of mismines (phrases and words I had incorrectly made into cards when they weren't actually the word being used in the sentence/I lacked the ability to correctly parse the sentence. By deleting your mining deck (every year or so, if you want to), you're potentially freeing yourself of a lot of words you never used anyway. Also, if deleting a card makes you forget a word, the card wasn't working in the first place ;P
  6. The Science of Letting Go / Tolerating Ambiguity / Narrow Reading, for when the going gets rough


Kana (phonetic building blocks)
  1. DJT kana game
  2. Kuuube's kana game
  3. Ved's kana game
  4. Tofugu's hiragana mnemonics
  5. Stroke order chart
  6. Tadoku readers (for practicing reading kana and furigana)
Vocabulary
  1. Before you do anything else, fix your font. You'll thank me later.
  2. Anki and its setup guide (flashcards, REQUIRED... well, highly recommended!)
  3. Kaishi 1.5k, an Anki vocab deck
  4. Manabi, another vocab deck (I recommend this after Kaishi.)
  5. Lapis, my favorite anki notetype
Grammar
  1. Yokubi, a grammar reference. Don't feel like you have to internalize this in one sitting. Good for beginners!
  2. Imabi. Speaks for itself.
  3. Bunpro grammar points
  4. Tae Kim's Grammar Guide
  5. JLPT Grammar Cheat Sheet
  6. Cure Dolly (bite-sized grammar videos) and a transcript of all her videos
Software / Extensions / etc
  1. Yomitan and its setup guide (or any other pop-up dictionary, pretty much REQUIRED)
  2. Preparing your PC for mining vocab
  3. GameSentenceMiner, a tool for immersing with visual novels and video games. Has Yomitan and OCR built-in. Saved my life. Note that I use this almost exclusively for VNs
  4. asbplayer, a browser extension that lets you mine vocab from anime subtitles
  5. The Subtitles In Question
  6. Manatan, a tool for immersing with anime, manga, light novels and EPUBs. Has built-in dictionary lookup. Extremely painless; I use this for manga and upload my own raws from local files
  7. Yatsu, an e-book reader, has really good features including device sync
  8. Hoshi, an iOS reader meant to emulate ttsu
  9. Yomine, something that might help you with mining from subtitles (if you're not sure what to mine)
Recommendations + Finding Content (immersion/"input")
  1. "What is immersion?" / the moe way guide
  2. Reading comprehension self-test
  3. Jiten.moe, a list of many books, shows, visual novels and video games with difficulties and personalized Anki decks (I love this site)
  4. VN Club, like jiten but just for VNs. Has great difficulty listings and cool tools
  5. Anikototv, an anime site where you can turn off subtitles and/or add your own with asbplayer
  6. JPDB, a similar site to jiten.moe
  7. Japanese Media Recommendations, a spreadsheet by kuri
  8. My (Bachelor's) VN recommendation list
  9. this big ass sheet of starter visual novels, video games and manga
  10. this VN chart (ordered by difficulty from easiest to hardest... note that some "average" games are actually very difficult and this is kind of an optimistic chart lol)
  11. So, how can I... acquire... visual novels in Japanese, anyway?
  12. Hakuneko has manga in JPN
  13. Anna's Archive has books
  14. It may seem crazy but this video improved my listening a lot.
Learning to Listen + Speak ("output")
  1. Kotu.io, a site with pitch accent recognition exercises and a mode where you can practice listening/participating in conversations
  2. Dogen, a popular YouTuber who frequently discusses pitch accent
  3. Learning to Output, a guide by Morg
  4. Nadeshiko, a sentence search engine (search for a word in English, Spanish or Japanese and get example sentences from real media)
  5. Massif.la, another sentence search engine (Nadeshiko skews more towards anime/manga, while Massif is sourced from 小説家になろう, a novel self-publishing site)
  6. xythh's pitch accent resources
  7. NotOrange's pitch notes
  8. Refold's output guide (multiple pages!)
  9. Chronopolize's output guide
  10. The Shadowing Textbook
  11. Anki and anime help, too. You tend to develop a natural ear for pitch if you know what you're listening for.
Miscellaneous
  1. Kanken deck (Anki), the easiest way to learn how to write. This deck assumes you already know how to read Japanese. Do that first.
  2. NihongoTracker, a browser-based immersion tracker with gamification elements. I use this literally every day.
  3. JLPT reading passages (similar to Tadoku readers)
  4. Practice with past JLPT exams
  5. Printable past N1 (JLPT) exams (to practice taking it physically; track time using a wristwatch or stopwatch)
  6. More JLPT practice tests
  7. Tangotype, for practicing typing in Japanese
  8. Fudoki, a guide to Japan's prefectures (in Japanese or English with a toggle button)
  9. Bookmeter, a site like goodreads/storygraph for tracking your reading
  10. practice speed-reading

  12. Japanese academic subjects
